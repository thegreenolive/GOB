# 04.04 Implementation Models

Implementation models illustrate the "how" of a solution. They show the computer's low-level perspective and the specific steps required to process data and obtain results. Since this type of model needs to specify decision-making, the notation and "grammar" are more elaborate than those of essential models. There are two types of implementation models considered in this section: **Flowcharts** and **Pseudocode**.&#x20;

### Flowcharts

Flowcharts can be considered "dynamic" diagrams because the audience can follow the program's logic by analyzing the program's flowchart. This methodology uses the following symbols:&#x20;

1. Oval (Termination): Represents the beginning (START) and the end (STOP) of a program. Every structured program should have exactly one of each.
2. Parallelogram (Input/Output): Used for all data entry and output processes, such as "Input num" or "Output sum".
3. Rectangle (Process): Used for arithmetic processes or assignments, such as SUM = SUM + NUM.
4. Diamond (Decision/Selection): Represents a juncture where logic paths diverge based on a condition. It must have at least three flow lines: one entering from the top and at least two leaving (Yes/No).
5. Flow Lines: Lines with arrows that indicate the direction of the logic flow.

#### Iteration Structures in Flowcharts

For cases where the program needs to repeat the same instructions until it achieves a certain objective, the decision block enables this iteration. The iteration can be accomplished in two ways: by asking the question before executing the corresponding instructions (pretest iteration) or after executing them (posttest iteration). In both cases, one of the answers brings back the flow to the same decision point. These iteration constructions are also known as _while_. Figure 04.08 shows the flowchart notation and examples for both types of while.

The first example, Figure 04.08, **Preparing sugared hot Tea for a friend**, includes the loop statement before the question. In this particular case, the assumption is that the server knows the friend wants sugar, but not how much. Therefore, at least one spoonful of sugar will be added to the drink, and more may be added until the friend considers it sufficient. This construct is the posttest while type.

In the second example, Figure 04.08, **Preparing sugared hot Tea for a friend (stirred version)**, the loop statements may be carried out, but only after the question. The server may not stir the Tea (if they consider the sugar dissolved immediately after adding it), or they may stir it several times. The idea of executing the loop statements only after asking the question in a program corresponds to the pretest _while_ construct.

It is important to craft the loop statements so that the flow can eventually exit the loop to avoid an **infinite loop**. Infinite loops take place when the loop statements do not guarantee that, eventually, the out-of-loop condition is met. In the last example, **Preparing sugared hot Tea for a friend (stirred version)**, if the loop statement does not involve a process to dissolve the sugar, there will be an infinite loop.
