# 02.2 Types of Models

Children are used to playing with models. Baby dolls, train sets, and toy kitchen sets are examples of physical models. Children use toys and experiment with them without risking damage to the real version. In real life, designers use physical models or prototypes to experiment with objects. For example, testing the aerodynamics of a motorcycle in a wind tunnel is usually done using physical models (see Figure 02.1).

Now, this guide focuses on abstract models. Abstract models are constructions that do not rely on the physical world but on ideas. There is no standard way to categorize abstract models, but in general terms, they fall into four categories:

* **Schematic Models**: These include pictures, schematics, charts, and graphics to visualize data or processes. A dynamic model explaining the pendulum mechanism of a clock (Figure 02.2) is a good example of a schematic model. Graphs are used to represent how changes in the magnitudes of variables affect one another. An example is used later in this section (Figure 02.4) to illustrate the relationship between the number of pies sold and a business's profit. Students are challenged to identify the kinds of simplifications the model in Figure 1 assumes.
* **Verbal Models**: These are narratives or written descriptions of a problem. For example, the capitalist model is an economic model based on private ownership of the means of production, competitive markets, and profit maximization. Most problem descriptions begin with a verbal model that is redefined and becomes a different type of model.

* **Mathematical Models**: These use constants, variables, and formulas to represent relationships. A mathematical model expresses associations among variables, and some of them can be considered universal. For example, Sir Isaac Newton described motion and the universal law of gravity using four equations (Figure 02.3).

• **Computer Models**: Also known as programs, are an elaborate version of mathematical models that seek to mimic the behavior of a natural phenomenon or process. The program is a set of instructions executed in a specific order to facilitate users' implementation of mathematical models. Weather prediction is usually done using computer models.
