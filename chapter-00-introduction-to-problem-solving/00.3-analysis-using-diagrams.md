# 00.3 Analysis using diagrams

The problem definition may not include all the factors that influence its circumstances since most problems have several aspects that cause or shape them. Using a diagram is a good way to depict problem-related factors. Usually, the factors included in a diagram can be categorized as static (components) and dynamic (processes). Some diagrams included a portrait of the dynamic factors, with a temporal component to indicate the priorities or the order of the processes affecting the problem.

For Problem 1, Lucy follows a specific order for her to arrive at her classes. We can use a diagram to describe the chain of events leading to her arrival (Fig. 1). Note that this diagram does not follow any specific notation. Later in this guide, you will be introduced to specific diagrams with well-defined notations.

This first diagram can help to pinpoint the process that needs our attention. The ideal path is shown in Figure 1, but alternative paths can be created from negative outputs at each stage. To simplify the example, let’s assume Lucy is using the bus system and taking the 7:15 AM bus, which should arrive at the University at 7:45 AM. Additionally, the bus stops are steps away from her apartment and the university’s building where she is taking classes, so walking to the bus stop from her apartment and to the classroom from the bus stop takes very little time. The diagram in Fig. 2 analyzes the possible outcomes of the entire process.

The new diagram has new elements. There is a new stage after the _'Wake up and get ready'_ ste&#x70;_: 'Arrive at Public Transportation on time'_. The _Commute_ element is broken into two: _The bus is on time_, and _The bus takes the expected time_. Some of those elements are included as decision points or outcomes in the flow. Also, the elements affected by Lucy’s waking up and getting ready are yellow, and the elements related to the transportation process are in blue.

The two main components of the diagram are mutually exclusive, since one process does not affect the other (it doesn’t matter if Lucy leaves her apartment early or late; that action does not affect that day's traffic). Also, they both cover the entire problem space, since there are no other elements in it.  Therefore, this diagram follows the MECE principle (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) to ensure it has no overlapping branches and covers the entire problem. When analyzing a problem, the MECE principle helps ensure that no possibilities or options are left out, conditional on our current understanding of the issue.

Diagram 2 has a [tree structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_structure). This type of diagram usually shows the hierarchy of elements within a topic/subject. Computer Science has its own definition of a [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_\(abstract_data_type\)), which is used to model data structures that support sorting and decision-making. The diagram used in this part of the problem-solving process depends on the type of problem. Nevertheless, a tree diagram is usually represented as a set of nodes and links. The nodes represent elements/processes, and the links show the relationships among them.
