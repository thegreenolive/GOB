# 00.5 Planning and analyzing

After identifying where to focus, a plan is needed to implement the solution. The leaves of the problem’s tree diagram identify the endpoints to solve. For Problem 1, the solution may not require an elaborate plan (getting a new alarm clock does not require complex planning. However, some solutions could need further analysis. Problem 2 provides a better example for this part.

{% hint style="danger" %}
Ray, a retired organic gardener with a "pollinator sanctuary" in his backyard, is having trouble keeping his flowers due to his neighbor’s decision to house a pack of active, large-breed rescue dogs. The dogs frequently run over his delicate flower beds, and the neighbor does not act upon her dogs. Therefore, Ray has engaged in repeated property line disputes.
{% endhint %}

<p align="center"><em><strong>Problem 2</strong></em></p>

The problem can be defined as: Ray needs a way to keep his flowers out of the reach of his neighbor’s dogs. The reader can work on Problem 2 through the steps described above as an exercise.\
After pruning and refining the problem, Ray concluded that he needs to create a physical barrier, a brick wall, between his property and his neighbor. Although the solution may seem straightforward, there are details that require careful planning. To carry out this project, Ray needs to survey his property, obtain local permits (HOA and/or city), locate utility lines, measure and calculate resources, procure materials, and perform the actual wall-building, including everything that entails. Figure 4 shows a diagram of the tasks required for wall construction and their dependency.

In Figure 4, the elements in yellow must be done before working on the green elements. Also, the tasks are presented so that the top ones have higher priority than the lower ones. Using the diagram, Ray could plan the wall construction and get a better idea of the order and the time required. The dependencies, responsibilities, and duration of each task should also be incorporated into the project plan. There are other factors to include in the planning section, such as risks, controls, and quality assurance.\[1]

There are other problems that do not have a clear and more efficient solution. Those problems are not singular. For this type of problem, the process may become even more iterative, and many potential solutions should be tested until the best solution is found.  Problem 3 is an example of this type.

{% hint style="danger" %}
Zoe has developed a fitness app, but data shows that 70% of users stop using it after three days.<br>
{% endhint %}

<p align="center"><em><strong>Problem 3</strong></em></p>

Zoe’s problem analysis is more complex than previous problems. A simple definition of the problem could be: “Zoe wants to lower the percentage of users who stop using her app after three days”. The app may face issues related to boarding, engagement, interface, competitors, social trends, or just user behavior/preferences. A priori, the problem cannot be limited to any single factor. Zoe needs to take a “hypothesis testing” approach. Figure 5 shows a diagram with factors that may influence customers to stop using the app (churn).

<figure><img src="../.gitbook/assets/Figure 5 Zoes app.png" alt="A hierarchical mind map branching from left to right, identifying three primary categories of friction that contribute to user churn for the &#x22;runur&#x22; app.  Main Root: &#x22;runur churn&#x22; (Grey box on the left). Each of the main branches of the diagram can be the origin of several hypotheses that must be tested before proceeding with the planned solution. Even more, the idea of a single, defined solution may not be adequate, since a combination of factors could make changes that improve several aspects of the problem. For example, after researching user expectations, Zoe may find that users fail to input their biometric data and skip the form. The solution to this problem will focus on Onboarding and Initial Friction, as well as Technical and User experience. The problem solver is responsible for prioritizing hypotheses and setting the corresponding tests before proceeding to work on a solution.
