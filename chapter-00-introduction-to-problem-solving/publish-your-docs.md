# 00.1 Problem-solving as a nonlinear process

Children are known for their stubbornness when trying to get what they want. Often, parents ask themselves how their child was able to open a container, break a toy, or lock herself inside a cabinet. The process children follow is iterative. They try something (sometimes without a clear purpose), and if the solution is not satisfactory, they try something else. This approach to finding a solution is like what has been termed “agile.”

According to the [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/) an Agile approach “responds to change over following a plan”.  Problem-solving is not a linear path from A to B, but rather an iterative cycle. There are straightforward problems, and for those situations, a single iteration is enough. Now, most complex scenarios, especially those involving multiple variables, actors, constraints, and deadlines, require several iterations to reach an acceptable solution. Using the child example from above, using iterations to find solutions contradicts the traditional "[Waterfall" approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall_model). A Waterfall approach assumes the existence of a linear plan that outlines a step-by-step approach to craft a solution to the given problem.

This guide will use a simplified version of the Seven-Steps Process\[1]—Define, Disaggregate, Prioritize, Plan, Analyze, Synthesize, and Communicate. This process is presented as a wheel to describe its dynamic character. If a satisfactory solution isn't found, the iteration’s result refines the problem statement or improves any other step, repeating the cycle until the solution is considered acceptable.
